Literary Contributions

Breakthrough Work: 7 Generations

In 2012, Robertson released 7 Generations: A Plains Cree Saga, a four-part graphic novel series. The story follows one Indigenous family over three centuries and seven generations. It was used in schools and libraries across Canada, where teachers and librarians credited it with inspiring young readers and sparking meaningful conversations about Indigenous history.

Language and Reclamation

When We Were Alone offers a concrete example of cultural reclamation. The book uses Cree words such as nókom, meaning grandmother, in a story about a child learning from her grandmother’s residential school experience. Robertson’s own father had been forbidden to speak Cree at day school. Printing Cree words in a children’s book makes language once suppressed by colonial education visible, readable, and available to another generation.

Career and Major Awards

Robertson has written more than 25 books, several of which are used in classrooms across Canada.

  1. 2017:When We Were Alone wins the Governor General's Literary Award, and Robertson receives the Beatrice Mosionier Aboriginal Writer of the Year Award.
  2. 2018:Strangers, the first Reckoner book, wins the McNally Robinson Best Book for Young People Award and the Michael Van Rooy Award for Genre Fiction at the Manitoba Book Awards. It also wins the Indigenous Writer category at the High Plains Book Awards.
  3. 2020:The Barren Grounds, the first book in The Misewa Saga, is nominated for the Governor General's Literary Award. Black Water: Family, Legacy and Blood Memory wins the Carol Shields Winnipeg Book Award and the Alexander Kennedy Isbister Award for Nonfiction and is named a Book of the Year by The Globe and Mail and Quill & Quire.
  4. 2021:On the Trapline wins the Governor General's Literary Award, making Robertson a two-time winner. He also receives the Writers' Union of Canada Freedom to Read Award.
  5. 2023:Robertson receives an Honorary Doctor of Letters from the University of Manitoba for his contributions to the arts.

His "Scaffolding" Approach

Robertson believes children should have access to books about difficult subjects, including colonialism, racism, and mental health. Rather than avoiding these topics, he introduces them in age-appropriate ways. This "scaffolding" approach lets young readers build a deeper understanding of complex issues as they grow older and read more of his work.
